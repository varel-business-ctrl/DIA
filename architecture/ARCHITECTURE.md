# DIA — Developmental Intelligence Architecture

## 1. Research Status

DIA is an experimental research architecture for investigating whether
intelligent behavior can emerge from a persistent adaptive system rather
than from a conventional language model alone.

DIA is a research hypothesis, not a claim that a new form of intelligence
has already been created.

---

## 2. Core Hypothesis

Intelligence may emerge from a persistent system that:

1. perceives an environment,
2. maintains internal state,
3. builds models of the environment,
4. remembers previous experience,
5. predicts possible outcomes,
6. acts on the environment,
7. observes consequences,
8. learns from prediction error,
9. generates its own experiments,
10. develops its internal organization over time.

---

## 3. Fundamental Principles

### Persistence

The organism continues to exist between individual interactions.

### Embodiment

The organism exists inside an environment and can interact with it.

### Perception

The organism receives observations rather than being given complete
knowledge of the environment.

### Memory

Experience can persist and influence future behavior.

### Prediction

The organism attempts to predict future observations and consequences.

### Action

The organism can intervene in its environment.

### Consequence

Actions produce consequences that can change both the environment and
the organism.

### Curiosity

The organism may choose actions because they are expected to produce
useful information or reduce uncertainty.

### Development

Experience can change the organism's internal organization.

### Evolution

Multiple organisms may eventually reproduce, mutate, compete,
cooperate, and develop across generations.

## 5. Major Components

### Perception

Converts environmental events into observations available to the
organism.

### Internal State

Maintains the organism's current computational condition.

Possible states include:

- stable
- uncertain
- curious
- focused
- exploring
- learning
- analyzing
- conflicted
- resting
- creating

These states are computational concepts and are not automatically
equivalent to human emotions.

### Memory

Initial memory systems:

- episodic memory
- semantic memory
- procedural memory

Future research may investigate:

- predictive memory
- self-memory
- hierarchical memory
- memory consolidation

### World Model

An internal representation of relationships, objects, events, rules,
and possible future states in the environment.

### Self Model

An internal representation of the organism itself, including:

- capabilities
- limitations
- internal state
- history
- actions
- consequences
- uncertainty

### Curiosity

A mechanism for selecting potentially informative experiences.

Possible factors:

- uncertainty
- novelty
- expected information gain
- prediction error

### Hypothesis Engine

Generates competing explanations for observations.

Example:

Observation:
An object changed position.

Possible hypotheses:

A. The object moved itself.
B. Another object moved it.
C. The environment changed.
D. The sensor produced an error.
E. An unknown mechanism caused the change.

The organism should be able to test hypotheses through intervention.

### Future Simulator

Simulates possible actions and consequences before committing to an
action.

### Decision Engine

Selects actions based on:

- predicted consequences
- uncertainty
- goals
- information value
- resource cost
- safety constraints

### Development Engine

Allows experience to modify:

- beliefs
- memories
- strategies
- representations
- models
- learning behavior

Future versions may investigate whether the system can modify parts of
its own architecture under strict safety constraints.

### Evolution Engine

Future generations may contain multiple organism variants.

Possible evolutionary mechanisms:

- mutation
- selection
- reproduction
- recombination
- competition
- cooperation

## 6. Artificial World

The first environment will be called:

WORLD-1

WORLD-1 will contain:

- objects
- resources
- obstacles
- energy
- spatial relationships
- changing conditions
- hidden rules
- other organisms

The organism will not initially receive complete knowledge of these
rules.

The purpose is to force learning through interaction.

---

## 7. First Organism

The first experimental organism will be:

O-001

O-001 will begin with minimal capabilities.

Initial capabilities:

- sensing
- movement
- basic memory
- prediction
- action
- learning

O-001 will initially have no requirement for human language.

---

## 8. Initial Actions

WORLD-1 actions:

- move_forward
- move_backward
- turn_left
- turn_right
- wait
- interact

More complex actions may be introduced only after the basic system
works.

---

## 9. Internal Representation

DIA must not assume that language is the fundamental representation
of intelligence.

Internal representations may include:

- vectors
- graphs
- symbolic structures
- temporal patterns
- probability distributions
- dynamical states
- spatial representations
- simulated futures

Language may eventually be added as an interface.

---

## 10. Learning

The initial learning mechanism will follow:

experience
→ prediction
→ action
→ consequence
→ prediction error
→ model update
→ memory update

A basic prediction error can be represented as:

e(t) = observation(t) - prediction(t)

The mathematical implementation will evolve as experiments reveal
limitations in the initial model.

---

## 11. Causal Learning

DIA must distinguish:

OBSERVATION

from

INTERVENTION

The organism should eventually learn questions such as:

"If I change A, does B change?"

Experiments should contain controls whenever practical.

Correlation alone must not be treated as proof of causation.

---

## 12. Scientific Method

Every major experiment should record:

- hypothesis
- system version
- environment
- initial conditions
- variables
- control
- intervention
- observations
- results
- statistical analysis
- alternative explanations
- failures
- next experiment

---

## 13. Anti-Self-Deception Rules

DIA must actively test whether apparent intelligence has simpler
explanations.

Examples:

Apparent intelligence
→ Could it simply be memorization?

Apparent curiosity
→ Could it simply be a reward function?

Apparent self-awareness
→ Could it simply be generated self-description?

Apparent creativity
→ Could it simply be recombination?

Apparent learning
→ Could it simply be parameter fitting?

Apparent evolution
→ Is the resulting complexity actually useful?

Apparent cooperation
→ Was cooperation explicitly programmed?

Claims must be supported by experiments.

---

## 14. Consciousness

DIA will not assume that it becomes conscious.

Consciousness is a separate research question.

Potential measurable properties for future investigation include:

- persistent self-model
- recurrent processing
- global information availability
- temporal integration
- metacognition
- self/other distinction
- flexible attention
- unified behavior

A system claiming to be conscious is not evidence that it is conscious.

---

## 15. Safety

Initial DIA systems must operate inside controlled environments.

Safety principles:

- sandboxed execution
- resource limits
- reproducible snapshots
- audit logs
- human approval gates
- restricted external access
- capability boundaries
- emergency shutdown
- immutable safety controls

DIA must not receive unrestricted control of:

- financial systems
- critical infrastructure
- weapons
- unrestricted computer networks
- physical machinery

## 17. Success Criteria

DIA should be evaluated on measurable capabilities:

- learning
- memory
- prediction
- adaptation
- exploration
- transfer
- causal reasoning
- generalization
- metacognition
- novel strategy generation
- development
- evolution
- collective intelligence

Performance must be compared against appropriate baselines.

---

## 18. Central Research Question

The central question of DIA is:

Can a persistent, embodied, adaptive computational system develop
increasingly general intelligent behavior through interaction,
prediction, memory, experimentation, and development?

The answer must be discovered experimentally.

DIA does not begin with the assumption that the hypothesis is true.

It begins by testing it.
